19. What Interviewers Are Really Testing

Why should I care?

GenAI and LLM engineering interviews focus on systems-level understanding rather than theoretical math proofs. Interviewers want to see that you can translate mathematical concepts into concrete engineering choices, manage memory limits, debug training instability, and design efficient serving systems.

Backend Engineer Lens

Think of this interview guide like preparing for a senior system design interview. Instead of simply memorizing the definitions of databases or caches, you explain *why* you chose a specific database (e.g. Postgres vs. Redis) based on its trade-offs. Similarly, you explain transformer components in terms of their system constraints (memory, compute, and latency).

Core Interview Topics

Topic 1: Attention Mechanics

What the interviewer is actually testing: Your understanding of information flow and memory scaling bottleneck ($O(T^2)$) in transformers.

Good Answer: Attention uses Query, Key, and Value matrices to compute similarity scores between tokens, allowing the model to focus on related words.

Strong Answer: Self-attention projects inputs into Queries, Keys, and Values. The dot product $Q K^T$ calculates similarity weights, which are scaled by $1/\sqrt{d}$ to prevent vanishing gradients, passed through a causal mask to block future tokens, and multiplied by $V$. Its memory footprint scales quadratically ($O(T^2)$), making long context lengths expensive. This bottleneck is optimized using FlashAttention (which computes attention in fast SRAM cache blocks) and GQA (which shares KV heads to reduce the KV Cache memory footprint during inference).

Common Mistakes: Saying FlashAttention is an approximation or drops tokens (it computes the exact same mathematical outputs as standard attention).

Topic 2: Gradient Accumulation

What the interviewer is actually testing: Your ability to manage hardware memory limits during training.

Good Answer: We split a large batch into smaller micro-batches and sum their gradients before updating parameters, simulating a larger batch size on limited hardware.

Strong Answer: We use gradient accumulation to support large effective batch sizes (e.g. $8,192$ tokens) when GPU memory is limited. We run $N$ forward/backward passes on smaller micro-batches, accumulating gradients in the parameters' `.grad` attributes before calling optimizer.step(). We must divide the loss by $N$ (grad_accum_steps) at each micro-step to normalize the accumulated gradients, preventing the effective learning rate from scaling by $N$.

Common Mistakes: Forgetting to normalize the loss, which destabilizes training and causes gradients to blow up.

Topic 3: Fine-Tuning & LoRA

What the interviewer is actually testing: Your understanding of model adaptation and parameter efficiency.

Good Answer: LoRA makes fine-tuning faster and cheaper by training small adapter layers instead of updating all model parameters.

Strong Answer: LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) freezes the pre-trained base model weights ($W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$) and decomposes weight updates into two low-rank matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$ (where rank $r \ll d$). During training, we only compute gradients and update optimizer states for these low-rank matrices, reducing memory overhead by up to 90% and enabling fine-tuning on consumer hardware.
