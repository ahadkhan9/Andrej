11. Experiments: Structural Interventions

Why should I care?

Understanding a system requires knowing not only how it works, but also how it fails. By examining what happens when core architectural components are removed or modified, you gain a deeper understanding of why they exist and the training failures they prevent.

Level 1 — Intuition & Mental Model

Think of the transformer architecture as a suspension bridge. Each steel cable, anchor point, and crossbeam has a specific purpose. If you remove a main cable (residual connection), the bridge collapses. If you remove the expansion joints (LayerNorm), temperature swings will warp the steel until it snaps. If you remove the road signs (position embeddings), drivers won't know which lane to use.

Level 2 — Engineering View: Failure Scenarios

We document six structural experiments, detailing their behaviors and failure modes:

1. Removing Positional Embeddings

Expected Behavior: The model trains and loss decreases slightly, but generated text becomes repetitive, grammatically incorrect, and loses coherence quickly. Downstream reasoning accuracy drops close to random chance.

Why it happens: Self-attention is permutation-invariant: it treats the input sequence as a "bag of words" without inherent order. Without positional embeddings, the model cannot distinguish word order.

What to observe in logs: Slower training loss decay, and repetitive generation loops (e.g. "the the the").

2. Removing Residual Connections

Expected Behavior: Training fails to converge. Gradients either vanish or explode, causing the model to return NaN.

Why it happens: Without residual connections ($x + f(x)$), gradients must flow back through a chain of multiplications of weights and activations. Early layers receive virtually no gradient signal during backpropagation.

What to observe in logs: Loss remains stuck at random baseline ($\approx 10.8$) or returns NaN within the first few steps.

3. Removing LayerNorm

Expected Behavior: Training fails immediately with loss returning NaN.

Why it happens: Without normalization layers, activation magnitudes grow exponentially as they pass through the blocks, causing arithmetic overflow (exceeding float range) during forward and backward passes.

What to observe in logs: Weight values and loss return NaN at step 0 or 1.

4. Removing Causal Masking

Expected Behavior: Training loss drops to near 0 quickly, but the model fails to generate coherent text during inference.

Why it happens: Without the causal mask, tokens can look ahead to future target tokens. The model learns to simply copy the next token from the input rather than learning semantic structures, making it useless for autoregressive generation.

What to observe in logs: Training loss decays abnormally fast to near 0, but generated outputs are repetitive loops of the prompt tokens.

5. Removing Weight Tying

Expected Behavior: Model trains successfully, but parameter count increases by $19.3\text{M}$ and training memory increases by $\approx 150\text{ MB}$.

Why it happens: Tying the weights of wte and lm_head shares their parameters. Untying them requires allocating separate memory for both layers' weights, gradients, and optimizer states.

What to observe in logs: Increased memory usage and slightly slower initial convergence.

6. Removing Gradient Clipping

Expected Behavior: Training runs normally for a few steps, but occasionally destabilizes, with loss spikes or NaN errors.

Why it happens: If the model encounters a steep region of the loss landscape, gradient values can grow extremely large. Without clipping, the optimizer takes an excessively large step, pushing weights to extreme values and causing the loss to explode.

What to observe in logs: Sudden spikes in gradient norm followed by loss returning NaN.

3. Mathematical View: Vanishing Gradients

The derivative of the loss $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to the input $x_0$ through $L$ non-residual blocks is: $$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x_0} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x_L} \prod_{l=1}^L \frac{\partial f_l(x_{l-1})}{\partial x_{l-1}}$$ If the Jacobian matrices $\frac{\partial f_l}{\partial x}$ have singular values less than 1.0, the product decays exponentially with depth ($L$), causing gradients in early layers to approach 0. Residual connections resolve this by introducing an identity term ($I$), ensuring stable gradient flow.
